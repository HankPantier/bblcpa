If you spent 40 hours this year running the concession stand at a Little League field, driving meals to homebound seniors through a Port Arthur church program, or serving on the board of a local nonprofit, the IRS thanks you for your service and gives you exactly zero dollars of deduction for your time. That surprises a lot of people. It also surprises people in the other direction: many volunteers assume none of their costs count, so they toss the gas receipts and never think about it again. Both assumptions are wrong, and the gap between them is where real tax savings sit.

The rule is narrower than most taxpayers think, but it's also more generous than most taxpayers realize. Your labor isn't deductible. The money you spend out of pocket to do that labor, for a qualified 501(c)(3) organization, often is. Here's how to tell the difference and keep records that hold up if the IRS ever asks.

## Your time has value, just not on Schedule A

The IRS is explicit about this in [Publication 526](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526): you cannot deduct the value of your time or services, no matter how skilled you are or what you'd normally charge for it. A dentist who spends a Saturday doing free cleanings at a community health fair can't deduct what those cleanings would have cost a paying patient. A CPA who prepares a nonprofit's Form 990 for free can't deduct her hourly billing rate either. The theory is simple: you never reported that income, so there's nothing to offset with a deduction.

What changes the math is money you actually spend from your own pocket to perform that volunteer work, with no reimbursement from the organization. That's where Publication 526 opens the door.

## The costs you can write off when you volunteer

Unreimbursed expenses that are directly connected to your volunteer service, and incurred only because of that service, are deductible as charitable contributions. Common examples include:

- Supplies you buy for the organization, like paint for a Habitat build or ingredients for a church fundraiser dinner
- Uniforms required for the volunteer role, such as a required scout leader uniform, as long as it isn't suitable for everyday wear
- Phone or postage costs incurred specifically for the charity's work
- Travel expenses when you're away from home overnight performing volunteer duties, including reasonable lodging and half the cost of meals
- Out-of-pocket costs of hosting a foreign exchange student placed by a qualified organization, up to $50 per month

The test is whether the expense exists because of the volunteer work and whether anyone reimbursed you for it. If your veterinary clinic donates its Saturday to a low-cost spay and neuter clinic and you personally buy extra surgical supplies the nonprofit didn't cover, that receipt counts. If the organization later reimburses you, it doesn't.

## Charitable mileage adds up faster than you think

Mileage is the deduction volunteers miss most often, and it's also one of the easiest to document. The IRS sets a flat charitable mileage rate that's fixed by statute, unlike the business and medical rates, which the IRS adjusts most years for inflation.

| Purpose | 2024 rate per mile |
|---|---|
| Charitable/volunteer driving | 14 cents |
| Business driving | 67 cents |
| Medical or moving driving | 21 cents |

Fourteen cents a mile looks small next to the business rate, but volunteer driving stacks up. A retiree who drives 15 miles round trip twice a week delivering meals for a Southeast Texas senior program logs roughly 1,560 miles a year, worth about $218 in deductible mileage. A real estate agent who drives to three Habitat for Humanity build sites a month racks up mileage on top of any tools or materials purchased. You can also deduct actual gas and oil costs instead of the standard rate, but not both, and either way parking fees and tolls are deductible separately.

## The expenses that don't qualify, even though they feel charitable

A few categories trip people up because they feel generous but fail the IRS test:

- The value of your time, professional services, or skilled labor, even at a documented hourly rate
- Childcare costs you pay so you can go volunteer
- Meals you eat while volunteering locally, unless you're away from home overnight on charity business
- Expenses for a family member who tags along but isn't actually performing volunteer duties
- Any cost that was reimbursed, or that you could have been reimbursed for and chose not to claim
- Donations of your time to organizations that aren't IRS-qualified 501(c)(3) charities, even if the cause is worthy

That last one matters more than people expect. Giving your time and money to a political campaign, a for-profit cooperative, or an informal mutual aid effort that never filed for 501(c)(3) status doesn't generate a deduction, no matter how much good it does. You can check an organization's status through the [IRS Charities & Nonprofits hub](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits) before you assume a contribution qualifies.

## Records the IRS wants before it believes you

Deductions for volunteer expenses get disallowed more often for weak paperwork than for bad-faith claims. Keep it simple but consistent:

- A mileage log with date, destination, purpose, and miles driven for each trip, not a single lump estimate at year end
- Receipts for supplies, uniforms, and travel costs, with a note connecting each purchase to the specific volunteer activity
- A written acknowledgment from the organization for any single unreimbursed expense of $250 or more, describing the services you provided and confirming you received no goods or services in return
- Bank or credit card statements as backup, especially for recurring costs like phone or printing expenses tied to a board role

A shoebox of gas receipts from December won't survive an inquiry. A simple spreadsheet updated monthly will.

## You have to itemize to claim any of this

Here's the catch that surprises even organized volunteers: none of these deductions do anything unless you itemize on Schedule A instead of taking the standard deduction. For 2024, the standard deduction is $14,600 for single filers and $29,200 for married couples filing jointly. If your total itemized deductions, including mortgage interest, state and local taxes, and charitable giving, don't clear that bar, your carefully logged volunteer mileage doesn't lower your tax bill at all.

This is exactly the kind of calculation that benefits from planning before year end rather than scrambling in April. High net worth individuals and retirees who volunteer regularly, especially those already itemizing because of mortgage interest or significant charitable giving, tend to get the most value from tracking these costs closely. Board members of local nonprofits face a related wrinkle. Serving on a board often means covering small recurring costs, travel to meetings, printing, the occasional working lunch, that add up over a year but rarely get tracked because they seem minor individually. Physicians and dentists who serve on hospital or association boards, and real estate professionals active in local trade groups, are especially prone to leaving these deductions on the table simply because nobody kept the receipts.

## Common Questions

**Can I deduct the value of professional services I donate to a nonprofit?**
No. The IRS specifically disallows a deduction for the value of your time or services, regardless of your profession or normal billing rate. You can only deduct unreimbursed out-of-pocket costs connected to performing that volunteer work, such as supplies or mileage.

**What's the mileage rate for volunteer driving in 2024?**
14 cents per mile, a rate set by statute rather than adjusted annually like the business mileage rate. You can also deduct actual gas and oil costs instead, plus parking and tolls either way, but you can't combine the standard rate with actual gas costs.

**Do I need a receipt for every volunteer expense I want to deduct?**
You need documentation for all of it, and a written acknowledgment from the organization specifically for any single unreimbursed expense of $250 or more, per [IRS Publication 526](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526). A mileage log and dated receipts are sufficient for smaller costs.

**Does volunteering for a friend's GoFundMe or informal fundraiser count?**
Only if the recipient organization is a qualified 501(c)(3). Time or money given to individuals, political campaigns, or unregistered groups doesn't qualify for a charitable deduction, even when the cause is genuinely charitable in nature.
