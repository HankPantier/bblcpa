Insurance salespeople sell policies. CPAs look at balance sheets. That difference matters more than most people realize, because the question "how much insurance coverage do you need" doesn't have a universal answer. It has a number, and that number comes from your net worth, your cash flow, and what you'd actually lose if something went wrong. A physician in Port Arthur with $2 million in retirement assets and a $400,000 mortgage needs a different risk profile than a retiree living on Social Security and a pension. Buying the same umbrella policy or the same disability rider for both would leave one person over-insured and paying for coverage they'll never use, and the other dangerously exposed.

That's the trap most insurance content falls into: generic checklists of "types of insurance" that treat every reader the same. This isn't that. It's a framework for figuring out where your actual exposure sits, so you stop paying premiums on risks that don't threaten you and start covering the ones that do.

## Start with net worth, not a policy checklist

Insurance exists to protect assets and income you can't afford to lose out of pocket. That means the starting point for any coverage decision is a real net worth statement, not a list of policy types pulled from a search result. If you have $150,000 in savings and a paid-off house, a $300,000 liability judgment could wipe you out. If you have $3 million in investable assets, that same judgment is a rounding error, but a lawsuit tied to a rental property or a professional decision could still reach into your other holdings if you're underinsured on liability.

Life insurance follows the same logic. A 45-year-old dentist earning $280,000 a year with 20 years left in practice is carrying roughly $5 million or more in future earning power, what actuaries call human capital. A term policy sized to replace two or three years of income doesn't come close to protecting a family from that loss. On the other hand, a retiree with no dependents and a fully funded pension often needs little to no life insurance at all, no matter what a captive agent's script says.

Disability insurance works the same way, and it's the coverage professionals underbuy most often. Physicians, dentists, and veterinarians derive nearly all their net worth from their ability to keep working. An own-occupation disability policy, one that pays out if you can't perform your specific specialty even if you could technically do other work, is worth far more to a surgeon or a large-animal vet than a standard any-occupation policy that assumes you could switch careers.

## Personal coverage gaps that put your net worth at risk

Homeowners and auto policies typically cap liability at $300,000 or $500,000. That ceiling was reasonable in 1985. It isn't today, especially for anyone with meaningful assets, a rental property, a boat, or a teenage driver. An umbrella policy that extends liability coverage to $1 million or $2 million usually costs $200 to $400 a year, and it's the single most underused tool for protecting net worth that's already been built.

High net worth individuals face a different version of this problem: too much coverage in low-risk areas, too little in concentrated ones. A collector car, a lake house, or a second property each carry distinct liability and property exposure that a standard homeowners policy doesn't fully address. If your household has multiple properties or significant investment income, a coordinated review, not a stack of separate policies bought at separate times, is what actually closes the gaps. Retirees have a narrower but real gap: long-term care and health-related costs that Medicare doesn't fully cover. Whether to self-insure that risk or buy a long-term care policy is a math problem tied to your investment portfolio and Social Security timing, not a decision to make off a brochure. ## A business insurance coverage checklist only gets you started

General liability, workers' compensation, and property coverage are the baseline for almost any Southeast Texas business. But the checklist stops being useful the moment your business has a professional license, employees, client data, or a physical location that can't shut down for a week without losing revenue. That's where a business insurance coverage checklist needs a second layer:

| Business type | Baseline coverage | Coverage often missed |
|---|---|---|
| Physician or dental practice | General liability, workers' comp | Malpractice tail coverage, cyber liability for patient records |
| Veterinary practice | Property, general liability | Business interruption, employment practices liability |
| Real estate brokerage | Auto, general liability | Errors & omissions (E&O), flood coverage on managed properties |
| Retail or restaurant | Property, workers' comp | Business interruption, liquor liability if applicable |

Cyber liability deserves a specific mention for medical and veterinary practices holding patient records, and for any business running payroll or client data through cloud systems. A single breach notification requirement under Texas law can cost more than a year of premiums for a policy that would have covered it.

Business insurance premiums are generally deductible as an ordinary business expense, which is worth factoring into the cost-benefit math rather than treating premiums as pure overhead. [IRS Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535) covers what qualifies. That deductibility doesn't mean buy more coverage than you need, but it does mean the real after-tax cost of adequate coverage is usually lower than the sticker price suggests.

## Real estate professionals carry concentrated, specific risk

Agents, brokers, and investors face liability exposure that doesn't show up on a generic checklist. Errors and omissions coverage protects against claims tied to disclosure mistakes or contract disputes, and it's not optional if you're licensed. Investors holding rental property in flood-prone parts of Jefferson County need separate flood coverage, since standard landlord policies exclude it entirely. Given how much of Southeast Texas sits in FEMA flood zones, this isn't a hypothetical add-on, it's often the single largest uncovered risk in a real estate professional's portfolio. ## Running your own insurance needs analysis

A useful needs analysis starts with three numbers: your net worth, your monthly cash flow if income stopped tomorrow, and the replacement cost of anything you couldn't rebuild out of pocket. From there:

1. List every asset a lawsuit or claim could reach, not just the one directly involved.
2. Calculate income replacement based on years until retirement, not a flat multiple of salary.
3. Match liability limits to your actual net worth, then add an umbrella policy to cover the gap.
4. Review business policies against your current revenue and headcount, since most coverage gaps come from policies that haven't been updated in three or four years.
5. Revisit the whole picture annually, alongside your tax planning, not as a separate errand.

That last step is where most people fall short. ## Common Questions

**How much life insurance coverage do I actually need?**
A reasonable starting point is 10 to 15 times your annual income for someone with 15 or more working years remaining and dependents relying on that income, adjusted down for existing savings and up for outstanding debt like a mortgage or practice loan. Someone within a few years of retirement, or without dependents, often needs far less.

**Do I need an umbrella insurance policy if I already have homeowners and auto coverage?**
Most households with a net worth above $300,000, a rental property, or a teenage driver benefit from one. Umbrella policies extend liability limits well beyond what standard auto and homeowners policies cap out at, usually for $200 to $400 a year for $1 million in additional coverage.

**What business insurance is legally required in Texas?**
Workers' compensation isn't mandatory for most private Texas employers, unlike in many states, but general liability and, for licensed professionals, malpractice or errors and omissions coverage are effectively required by lenders, landlords, and licensing boards even without a state mandate.

**Is business insurance tax deductible?**
Most ordinary business insurance premiums, including liability, property, and workers' compensation, are deductible as a business expense under [IRS guidelines](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535). Deductibility shouldn't drive the coverage decision, but it does lower the real cost of carrying adequate protection.
