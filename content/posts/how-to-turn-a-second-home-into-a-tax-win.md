A lake house on Toledo Bend or a condo in Galveston sounds like a lifestyle upgrade, and it is one. But the IRS doesn't care about your view. It cares about how many nights you slept there versus how many nights you rented it out, and that one number decides whether your second home behaves like a tax shelter or turns into a rental business with its own set of rules. Get the split right and you can write off mortgage interest, property tax, and even a chunk of your rental losses. Get it wrong and you could lose deductions you assumed were automatic.

This matters more right now because mortgage rates have kept a lot of would-be second-home buyers on the sidelines, and the ones who do buy are increasingly renting the property out part of the year to offset the payment. That single decision, how many days you rent versus how many days you or your family use it, changes your tax picture completely.

## The 14-day rule that changes everything

Here's the rule that trips up almost everyone: if you rent your second home for 14 days or fewer during the year, the IRS doesn't consider you a landlord at all. You don't report the rental income. Not one dollar of it. You still deduct mortgage interest and property taxes as you would on any second residence, but the rental side of the ledger stays completely off your return.

Cross that 14-day threshold and the property flips into rental territory, subject to the rules in [IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527). Now you report the income, but you can also deduct a proportional share of expenses like utilities, insurance, repairs, cleaning fees, and depreciation. The catch is that your personal use starts limiting how much of those expenses you can deduct.

## Personal use versus rental use, and why the ratio matters

Once you're past 14 rental days, the IRS wants to know how the property was used all year, and it separates it into three buckets: personal-use days, rental days at fair market value, and days it just sat empty.

If your personal use exceeds the greater of 14 days or 10% of the total days rented, the property is treated as a personal residence with rental use attached. That triggers a specific limitation: your rental expense deductions can't exceed your rental income. You can still deduct mortgage interest and taxes in full as itemized deductions, but rental losses beyond your rental income get carried forward instead of offsetting your other income this year.

Stay under that personal-use threshold, and the property is treated as a rental property. That opens the door to deducting a rental loss against other income, subject to the usual passive activity limits, and depreciation becomes a real annual write-off rather than a carryforward.

| Personal use days | Rental days | Tax treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Any amount | 14 or fewer | No rental income reported, mortgage interest and taxes fully deductible |
| More than greater of 14 days or 10% of rental days | 15+ | Treated as personal residence, rental losses capped at rental income |
| 14 days or fewer (or under 10% of rental days) | 15+ | Treated as rental property, losses may offset other income |

## Second home versus investment property, and why lenders and the IRS disagree

Your mortgage lender and the IRS don't always define "second home" the same way. A lender might approve you for a second-home loan with a lower rate as long as you occupy the property some part of the year and don't rent it out full time as a business. The IRS, on the other hand, only cares about the actual day count once tax season arrives. A property financed as a second home can still function as a rental property on your tax return if you cross the rental-day thresholds. Conversely, a property marketed as an investment property but used personally for a few weeks a year might still trigger personal-use limitations you didn't plan for.

The practical takeaway: don't let the loan paperwork dictate your tax strategy. Track your actual usage days from day one, in a spreadsheet, an app, or a simple calendar note each time you or a renter stays there.

## What actually counts as a personal-use day

A lot of second-home owners miscount this and it costs them. A day counts as personal use if you, a family member, or anyone paying less than fair market rent stays there, even if you're doing repairs for part of the day. There's one exception worth knowing: days spent primarily on repairs and maintenance don't count as personal use, even if you sleep there overnight, as long as the work is the main purpose of the trip. Keep receipts and a simple log if you're leaning on that exception, because it's the kind of detail that matters if the return gets a second look.

## Depreciation is a bigger lever than most owners realize

Once a second home qualifies as a rental property, depreciation becomes one of the largest annual deductions available, often outpacing mortgage interest in the early years of ownership. The building's cost basis, not the land, gets depreciated over 27.5 years for residential rental property. On a $400,000 vacation condo with a $300,000 building allocation, that's roughly $10,900 a year in depreciation, real money reducing your taxable rental income even in years when cash flow is positive.

The rules covering vacation home rentals, including how to allocate expenses between personal and rental use, are laid out in [IRS Tax Topic 415](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc415). It's dense reading, which is exactly why owners bring it to a CPA rather than trying to self-diagnose it every April.

## Common Questions

**What is the IRS 14-day rule for a second home?**
If you rent your second home for 14 days or fewer in a tax year, you don't have to report any of that rental income, regardless of how much you charged. You still deduct mortgage interest and property taxes as you normally would on a personal residence.

**How is a second home taxed differently than an investment property?**
A second home used mostly for personal enjoyment, with rental days under the 14-day or 10% thresholds, keeps favorable personal-residence deductions. Cross those thresholds and the IRS treats it more like a rental business, capping loss deductions or opening up fuller rental deductions depending on how much you personally use it, per [IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527).

**Do I owe taxes on vacation home rental income?**
Yes, once you exceed 14 rental days in the year, all rental income becomes taxable, though you can offset it with a proportional share of expenses like utilities, repairs, insurance, and depreciation based on your rental-to-personal-use ratio.

**Can I deduct a loss on my second home rental?**
Only if your personal use stays at or below the greater of 14 days or 10% of total rental days. Exceed that and your rental expense deductions are capped at your rental income for the year, with any excess carried forward instead of offsetting other income.
