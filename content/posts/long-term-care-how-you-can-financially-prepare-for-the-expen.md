Nobody plans a coffee meeting to talk about nursing homes. But if you're 55 or older, or you've got parents who are, long-term care is probably already sitting in the back of your mind, right next to "did I save enough for retirement?" Here's the uncomfortable math: a private room in a Texas nursing facility can run well over $8,000 a month, and home health aides aren't cheap either. Medicare generally does not cover extended custodial care. That gap between what you'll need and what's automatically covered is exactly where a financial plan has to do the heavy lifting, years before anyone dials a facility's admissions office.

We treat long-term care the same way we treat any other financial risk: something to fund deliberately, not something to figure out in a crisis. That means blending insurance, veterans benefits, and personal savings into a plan that matches your specific balance sheet, not a generic checklist pulled from a magazine.

## Why the price tag catches families off guard

Most people budget for retirement using the cost of living they already know: the mortgage, groceries, travel, maybe a grandkid's tuition fund. Long-term care doesn't show up in that budget because it isn't a guaranteed expense. Roughly 7 in 10 people turning 65 will need some form of long-term care in their lifetime, according to federal aging data, yet very few build a specific savings bucket for it.

The other surprise is duration. Care needs aren't a one-time bill. Home health support might run a few hours a day for two years. Memory care can stretch past five years. Multiply either scenario by a monthly cost in the thousands, and you're looking at a six-figure liability that either gets funded on purpose or gets funded by liquidating a retirement account at the worst possible time, often triggering a tax bill you didn't plan for either.

## Three ways to pay and how they actually stack up

Most funding strategies fall into three buckets. Few people rely on just one.

| Funding method | Best fit | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term care insurance | Clients in their 50s and early 60s in good health | Premiums rise with age; underwriting gets tougher after a diagnosis |
| VA Aid and Attendance benefit | Wartime veterans and surviving spouses who need help with daily activities | Net worth limits and a look-back period on asset transfers |
| Self-funding from savings and investments | High net worth households and those who couldn't get insured | Requires disciplined, separate earmarking, not just "we'll figure it out" |

Insurance locks in a known monthly premium in exchange for a defined benefit later, but it only works if you buy it while you're still insurable. Waiting until your 70s, or until after a health scare, usually means denial or premiums that no longer make financial sense. Self-funding gives you total control and no premium payments, but it demands real discipline: the money has to be treated as untouchable, not folded into your general investment portfolio and rebalanced away when the market gets rocky. If you haven't looked at how your portfolio is allocated in a while, it's worth revisiting how [a balanced investment portfolio](/resources/strategically-balancing-your-investment-portfolio-why-set-it) should actually be managed before assuming it will double as a care fund.

## How the VA Aid and Attendance benefit works

For veterans and surviving spouses, this benefit is one of the most underused resources in long-term care planning. The [VA Aid and Attendance and Housebound benefit](https://www.va.gov/pension/aid-attendance-housebound/) adds a monthly payment on top of a veteran's pension for those who need help with daily activities like bathing, eating, or managing medications, or who are largely confined to their home.

The benefit isn't automatic. It requires a pension application, medical documentation, and attention to net worth limits and a look-back period on gifted or transferred assets. Families who wait until a crisis, say, a sudden hospitalization, often lose months of eligible benefits simply because the paperwork wasn't started early. If there's a veteran or surviving spouse in your family, this is worth raising with your CPA or elder law attorney well before care is needed, not after the ambulance ride.

## Where self-funding and tax planning intersect

Self-funding isn't just "having enough money." It's structuring your accounts so that care expenses don't blow up your tax picture. A few specifics worth knowing:

- Qualified long-term care insurance premiums and unreimbursed long-term care services can count as deductible medical expenses if you itemize and your total medical costs clear the AGI threshold, as outlined in [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502).
- Health savings account funds can be used tax-free for qualifying long-term care expenses, which makes an HSA one of the more flexible self-funding tools if you're still contributing during your working years.
- Retirement account withdrawals to cover care costs are still taxable income. Timing those withdrawals against other income in a given year matters, particularly for retirees managing Social Security taxation and Medicare premium brackets.

This is where a CPA earns their keep. It's not enough to have savings; the sequence in which you tap taxable accounts, tax-deferred accounts, and Roth accounts to pay for care can shift your tax bill by thousands of dollars a year. For retirees already juggling required minimum distributions and investment income, this becomes part of the same year-round conversation we have around [tax planning for retirees](/industries/retirees).

## Building the plan before you need it

The window for buying insurance affordably and applying for VA benefits without a scramble is narrower than most people think. A rough guide:

- **Mid-50s to early 60s:** Best window to shop long-term care insurance while premiums and underwriting are still favorable.
- **Late 60s:** Time to formalize a self-funding earmark if insurance is no longer cost-effective, and to confirm VA eligibility documentation is current.
- **Any age, once a diagnosis or care need appears:** Focus shifts from acquiring coverage to funding sequence, tax timing, and benefit applications.

High net worth households sometimes assume they can simply self-fund without a formal plan. That works, but only if the earmarked assets are actually protected from market swings and identified separately from retirement income. We walk through this kind of structuring regularly with clients through our [high net worth individual](/industries/high-net-worth-individuals) planning work, where long-term care sits alongside estate and tax strategy rather than as an afterthought.

If you're the adult child managing this conversation with a parent, the same logic applies, just compressed. Ask what's already in place: a policy, a VA claim, a specific savings account. If the answer is "we'll cross that bridge when we get there,” that bridge usually gets crossed during a hospital stay, which is the worst time to be reading insurance fine print or gathering discharge paperwork for a VA claim.

## Common Questions

**Is long-term care insurance worth it if I'm already 68?**
It can still make sense, but premiums will be higher and underwriting stricter, especially with existing health conditions. At that stage, we often compare a hybrid life insurance policy with a long-term care rider against a straightforward self-funding plan, since the math doesn't always favor traditional LTC insurance once you're past your mid-60s.

**Can I use my HSA to pay for a parent's nursing home costs?**
Generally, HSA funds can only be used tax-free for the account holder's own qualifying medical and long-term care expenses, not a parent's, unless the parent is claimed as a tax dependent. This is a common point of confusion worth confirming with your CPA before you assume the funds are available.

**Does the VA Aid and Attendance benefit cover home health aides?**
Yes. The benefit is designed for veterans and surviving spouses who need help with daily activities, whether that care happens at home, in assisted living, or in a nursing facility, as long as eligibility requirements around service history, medical need, and net worth are met.

**How much should I actually set aside if I plan to self-fund?**
A reasonable starting point for a single person is enough to cover two to three years of care at current local facility rates, adjusted upward for inflation in health costs, which historically runs higher than general inflation. Your specific number depends on your health history, family longevity, and what other income sources, like a pension or VA benefit, would offset the cost.
