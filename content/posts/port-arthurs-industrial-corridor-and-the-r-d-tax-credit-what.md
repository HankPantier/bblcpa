Drive down Procter Street or Highway 82 in Port Arthur and you're never far from a flare stack or a cooling tower. Motiva, Valero, and TotalEnergies keep the local economy running, but they don't run it alone. Behind every turnaround, unit expansion, and process upgrade sits a web of smaller companies: fabrication shops, instrumentation contractors, specialty coating crews, industrial suppliers, and process engineers who never touch a beaker but solve technical problems for a living. A surprising number of these businesses are doing R&D-qualifying work every week and leaving the federal research credit on the table because nobody told them it applied to them.

## The credit rewards problem-solving, not lab coats

The research and development tax credit, created under Internal Revenue Code Section 41, has an image problem. Say "R&D credit" and most business owners picture a pharmaceutical lab or a software startup. That's not how the law is written. The credit applies to any business that spends time and money trying to improve a product or process and isn't sure, going in, whether the approach will work. A machine shop redesigning a fixture to hold tighter tolerances for a refinery client qualifies just as easily as a chemist formulating a new compound. We've written before about how [restaurants, farms, and manufacturers across Southeast Texas often qualify for R&D tax credits](/resources/myth-busting-you-dont-need-to-be-a-tech-company-to-claim-r-d) even though almost none of them think of themselves as "research" companies. Fabricators and process contractors in Jefferson County fall into that same blind spot, and it's an expensive one.

## Who along the supply chain typically qualifies

The companies most likely to have a real claim are the ones doing hands-on technical problem-solving for the plants, not the ones simply supplying commodity parts off a shelf. Think about the work that actually happens in a given month:

- A fabrication shop developing a new weld procedure to join dissimilar metals for a high-pressure line
- An instrumentation contractor building custom control logic to integrate new sensors into an existing plant network
- A coatings applicator testing corrosion-resistant materials for equipment exposed to sour gas service
- A machine shop engineering a replacement part with tighter tolerances because the original manufacturer no longer exists
- A process contractor redesigning a piping configuration to reduce downtime during a turnaround

None of that looks like a laboratory. All of it involves testing, iteration, and genuine technical uncertainty, which is exactly what the credit is built to reward. Manufacturers and processors are one of the niches we work with most often at Brammer, Begnaud, & Lattimore, and it's rare to sit down with one and not find at least one project from the past year that would hold up under the credit's four-part test.

## What the work has to look like to qualify

The IRS uses a four-part test to decide whether a project counts as qualified research. All four parts have to be met, but they're less restrictive than most owners assume.

| Test | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| Permitted purpose | The work aims to improve a product, process, technique, formula, or software |
| Technological in nature | The work relies on principles of engineering, physics, chemistry, or a similar hard science |
| Elimination of uncertainty | At the start, you didn't know whether the approach would work or how to achieve the result |
| Process of experimentation | You tested, modeled, or evaluated more than one approach to get there |

A project doesn't need a patent, a published paper, or a dedicated R&D department. It needs documented trial and error aimed at solving a technical problem. Failed attempts count too. If a fabrication shop tried three different weld sequences before one held up under pressure testing, the two that failed are part of the qualifying activity, not a reason to leave it off the claim.

## Jefferson County's dual credit opportunity

Most of the conversation around this credit focuses on the federal side, claimed on [IRS Form 6765, Credit for Increasing Research Activities](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-6765). That's the main event, and for a fabrication or process contracting business with, say, $400,000 in qualifying wages tied to engineering and technical staff, the federal credit alone can run into five figures.

But Texas businesses have a second option worth checking. The state offers qualifying research businesses a choice between a franchise tax credit or a sales and use tax exemption on equipment used in the research, administered through the [Texas Comptroller's office](https://comptroller.texas.gov/taxes/). A business can't claim both for the same activity, but choosing the right one matters, especially for a company buying specialized test equipment or fabrication tooling as part of its R&D work. For a manufacturer in Jefferson County running lean on margin, that election can be the difference between a modest tax credit and a meaningful reduction in equipment costs the year they're incurred.

## Common Questions

**Does a business need a dedicated engineering department to claim the R&D credit?**
No. A dedicated department helps with documentation, but the credit is based on the activity itself, not the org chart. A three-person fabrication shop where the owner and two welders spend part of their week solving a technical problem for a refinery client can qualify the same as a 50-person engineering firm.

**Can a business claim the credit for a project that failed?**
Yes. Failed experiments are strong evidence of the technical uncertainty the IRS looks for. Wages and costs tied to an approach that didn't work still count toward the credit as long as the project overall meets the four-part test.

**How far back can a Jefferson County business claim missed R&D credits?**
Generally, businesses can amend prior year returns and claim the credit for up to three open tax years, depending on when each return was filed. That means a manufacturer that's been doing qualifying work since 2022 without claiming it may still have a window to recover credits from those earlier years.

**Does claiming the federal R&D credit affect a company's Texas franchise tax?**
Only indirectly. The federal credit and the Texas research credit are separate elections calculated under different rules. A business can claim the federal credit on Form 6765 and separately evaluate whether the Texas franchise tax credit or sales tax exemption makes more sense for its Texas activity.
