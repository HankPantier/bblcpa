Here's a question worth asking at your next portfolio review: when did you last check whether your allocation still matches your goals? If the honest answer is "not since I opened the account," you're not alone, but you're also probably leaving money on the table. Markets move, life changes, and tax law shifts every year. A portfolio built with the right balance in 2020 can look nothing like the right balance today, even if you never placed a single trade.

That's the trap of "set it and forget it." It sounds disciplined. In practice, it just means your risk exposure is drifting on autopilot, usually in the direction of whatever asset class has been winning lately. For physicians, real estate investors, and retirees living off a portfolio in Southeast Texas, that drift has real consequences: bigger swings than you signed up for, missed tax opportunities, and sometimes a much rockier retirement than the one you planned.

## Why your portfolio drifts even when you never touch it

Asset allocation is a snapshot, not a permanent setting. Say you build a 60/40 stock-to-bond portfolio because that split matches your risk tolerance and time horizon. Three strong years for equities later, that same account might sit closer to 72/28 without a single dollar added or withdrawn. Nobody decided to take on more risk. The market decided for you.

The Federal Reserve's research on household finance and investment behavior points to a related pattern: households often underestimate how much their risk exposure has shifted after a sustained market run, and they react to volatility only after it's already hurt them. That's the cost of skipping the periodic check-in. A diversified portfolio that isn't periodically rebalanced back to target isn't really diversified anymore; it's just whatever the market decided to leave you with. Read more from the [Federal Reserve's notes on household finance and investment behavior](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/default.htm).

## Risk tolerance changes with your life, not just your mood

Risk tolerance questionnaires treat risk appetite like a personality trait: fixed, static, answered once at account opening. It isn't. A dentist five years from selling her practice has different liquidity needs than one who just signed a new lease on a second location. A retiree drawing Social Security and required minimum distributions needs a different mix than someone still ten years from retirement. The right allocation strategy has to flex with income stability, time horizon, and what the money is actually for.

| Client situation | What tends to shift | Allocation consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Physicians building wealth mid-career | Income is high and stable, time horizon is long | Higher equity weighting can tolerate short-term swings |
| Real estate professionals with concentrated property holdings | Wealth is already tied to one asset class | Portfolio diversification matters more, not less |
| Retirees drawing on the portfolio for income | Sequence-of-returns risk becomes the main threat | Shift toward capital preservation and cash flow timing |
| High net worth individuals with multiple income sources | Tax bracket and estate considerations grow more complex | Rebalancing decisions get evaluated for tax impact first |

The table isn't a formula to copy. It's a reminder that a balanced investment portfolio for a [physician](/industries/physicians) building wealth at 42 should not look like the one that makes sense for a [retiree](/industries/retirees) drawing income at 72, even if both started with the same textbook 60/40 split a decade ago. [Real estate professionals](/industries/real-estate-professionals) in particular often over-concentrate without realizing it, since so much of their net worth already sits in property. And for [high net worth individuals](/industries/high-net-worth-individuals), the rebalancing conversation is inseparable from the tax conversation, which brings us to the part most DIY investors skip entirely.

## Rebalancing without handing the IRS an extra check

Here's where a lot of good intentions go sideways. An investor notices their portfolio has drifted, decides to fix it, and sells a chunk of appreciated stock in a taxable brokerage account to buy back into bonds. That triggers a capital gains bill that could have been avoided.

A few tax-efficient rebalancing moves worth understanding:

- **Rebalance inside tax-advantaged accounts first.** Trades inside a 401(k) or IRA don't trigger capital gains, so that's often the cheapest place to correct drift.
- **Direct new contributions toward the underweighted asset class** instead of selling winners. If your target is 60/40 and you're sitting at 68/32, sending new retirement contributions into bonds for a while can close the gap without a single taxable sale.
- **Pair rebalancing with tax-loss harvesting.** If part of the portfolio needs trimming anyway, selling a position at a loss to offset a gain elsewhere can turn a rebalancing trade into a tax deduction instead of a tax bill.
- **Watch the calendar around required minimum distributions.** For retirees, an RMD is sometimes the easiest built-in rebalancing tool available, since the withdrawal itself can be structured to pull from the overweighted asset class.

The AICPA's Personal Financial Planning resources cover this kind of coordinated tax and investment planning in more depth, and it's worth understanding before your next trade, not after. ## Common Questions

**How often should I rebalance my investment portfolio?**
Most financial planners suggest reviewing allocation at least annually, though a semiannual check catches drift before it compounds. The right frequency also depends on how volatile your holdings are; a portfolio heavy in individual stocks or concentrated real estate needs more frequent attention than one built mostly on broad index funds.

**Does rebalancing always trigger a taxable event?**
No. Rebalancing inside a 401(k), IRA, or other tax-advantaged account doesn't create a tax bill, since those trades aren't taxed until withdrawal. Taxable brokerage accounts are where the strategy matters most, which is why directing new contributions and pairing sales with tax-loss harvesting can keep the tax hit to a minimum.

**What's the difference between asset allocation and diversification?**
Asset allocation is the split between broad categories like stocks, bonds, and cash. Diversification is what happens within each category, spreading holdings across sectors, geographies, or property types so a single bad outcome doesn't sink the whole portfolio. You need both working together for a portfolio that actually protects wealth.
