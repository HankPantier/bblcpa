If you've sat in a dealership finance office in Port Arthur or Beaumont and watched the salesperson slide a lease quote across the desk right after a purchase quote, you already know the pitch: lower payment, newer car every few years, no worries about resale. What they don't walk you through is what that decision does to your tax return, your cash flow, or your balance sheet if the vehicle is tied to a business. That's the piece we look at with clients every day, and it's usually the piece that actually decides the answer.

There's no universal right answer between leasing and buying a car. There is a right answer for your situation, and it depends on how you use the vehicle, how you file your taxes, and how long you plan to keep it. Here's the framework we use with physicians, dentists, real estate agents, and small business owners across Southeast Texas when this question comes up.

## The math dealerships rarely walk you through

Monthly payment is the number every ad leads with, and it's the least useful number for deciding anything. A lease payment looks smaller because you're only financing the depreciation during the term, plus interest and fees, not the full price of the car. Buying finances the whole vehicle, so the payment is higher, but you're building equity every month instead of handing the car back at lease-end with nothing to show for it.

The real comparison has to include [total cost of ownership](/resources/do-the-math-behind-renting-vs-buying-a-home) over the time you'd actually keep the vehicle, not just the monthly line item. That means adding up the down payment, all monthly payments, mileage penalties, wear-and-tear charges, and either the trade-in value (if you buy) or the disposition fee (if you lease and walk away).

| Factor | Leasing | Buying |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly payment | Lower, typically | Higher, typically |
| Upfront cost | Lower or none | Down payment, taxes, fees |
| Mileage limits | Usually 10,000–15,000/year | None |
| End of term | Return car, no equity | You own the asset |
| Repairs after warranty | Rarely your problem | Your responsibility |
| Best for | Drivers who swap cars every 2–3 years and stay under mileage caps | Drivers who keep vehicles 5+ years or drive high mileage |

If you're the type who keeps a truck for eight years and puts 20,000 miles a year on it for property showings or house calls, leasing will cost you in mileage overages what it saved you in monthly payments. If you like a new vehicle every three years and drive a predictable, modest number of miles, leasing can be the cheaper path.

## Business use changes the entire equation

Everything above assumes a personal vehicle. Once a car, truck, or SUV is used for business, whether that's a real estate agent driving to listings, a veterinarian making farm calls, or a contractor hauling equipment, the tax code adds a second layer that often outweighs the first.

Buying a vehicle used for business opens the door to Section 179 expensing and bonus depreciation, which can let you deduct a large chunk of the purchase price in the year you buy it, subject to IRS limits on vehicle weight and business-use percentage. The [IRS Publication 946 guidance on depreciating property](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946) lays out exactly how those limits work, including the lower caps that apply to passenger vehicles versus heavier trucks and SUVs used over 50% for business. A vehicle with a gross weight over 6,000 pounds, common with many trucks and larger SUVs, typically qualifies for a much larger first-year deduction than a sedan.

Leasing a business vehicle works differently. Instead of depreciating the asset, you deduct the business-use portion of your lease payments each year, plus an "inclusion amount" the IRS requires for higher-value leased vehicles to keep the playing field level with buyers. It's a steadier, smaller deduction spread over the lease term rather than one large hit up front.

Neither approach is automatically better. A physician buying a heavy SUV for practice-related travel might get a bigger first-year write-off by purchasing. A real estate agent who trades vehicles every two years and wants predictable expenses might prefer the leasing deduction. The right call depends on your income this year, your expected income next year, and whether you'd rather have the deduction now or spread out. This is exactly the kind of calculation we run for clients before they sign anything, not after.

## Cash flow and credit matter as much as the tax return

Auto debt is not a small line item in most households or business budgets. Outstanding auto loan balances nationally sit in the hundreds of billions of dollars, and the [Federal Reserve's G.19 Consumer Credit report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g19/current/) tracks how that debt, and the interest rates attached to it, moves over time. When rates rise, the gap between a lease payment and a loan payment widens, because leasing insulates you from some of that rate exposure while a longer loan term locks you into it.

For a small business owner, the cash flow question isn't just the payment size. It's what else that cash could be doing. If tying up capital in a down payment means delaying payroll software, inventory, or a marketing push, financing that frees up cash today, even at a slightly higher total cost, can be the smarter move for the business as a whole. This is the kind of decision that looks different on a spreadsheet than it does in a dealership showroom, which is exactly why we build it into the CFO-level guidance we provide clients through our [Business Management Services](/industries/real-estate-professionals).

## Which niches tend to lean which way

A few patterns show up consistently in our client work across Southeast Texas.

Real estate professionals who put heavy mileage on a vehicle for showings, inspections, and client meetings usually come out ahead buying, especially if the vehicle qualifies for Section 179. The deduction plus the mileage flexibility outweighs the lower lease payment almost every time. We walk through this in detail with agents and brokers on our [real estate professionals page](/industries/real-estate-professionals).

Physicians and dentists with more predictable, lower-mileage driving patterns, and higher income that benefits from spreading deductions evenly, sometimes do better leasing a practice vehicle and taking the straightforward payment deduction rather than managing depreciation schedules. Details specific to practice owners are on our [physicians page](/industries/physicians).

Retirees and high net worth individuals buying a personal vehicle, with no business-use deduction at stake, should focus purely on how long they'll keep the car and what their credit terms look like. If you plan to keep a vehicle past the loan term, buying nearly always wins on total cost.

Small business owners weighing a fleet decision, multiple trucks or vans instead of one vehicle, benefit from running the Section 179 math across the whole group before committing to lease or buy on any single unit, since limits and phase-outs apply at the business level, not per vehicle.

## Common Questions

**Is it better to lease or buy a car for tax purposes?**
It depends on business use. A vehicle used more than 50% for business can qualify for Section 179 expensing if purchased, often producing a larger first-year deduction, while a leased business vehicle generates a smaller, steadier deduction based on the business-use percentage of your lease payments. Personal vehicles with no business use don't get either benefit, so the decision comes down to total cost over time rather than tax treatment.

**Can I deduct my car lease if I'm self-employed?**
Yes, the business-use portion of your lease payments is deductible, but the IRS requires an income inclusion amount for higher-value vehicles to prevent leasing from becoming a tax-avoidance shortcut. The exact math depends on the vehicle's fair market value and your business-use percentage, so it's worth confirming the calculation before you file.

**Does Section 179 apply to leased vehicles?**
No. Section 179 expensing applies to vehicles you purchase and own, not lease. If you lease, you deduct payments as a business expense instead, following the rules outlined in [IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946).

**How many miles do I need to drive to make buying worth it?**
There's no single number, but most lease agreements cap mileage between 10,000 and 15,000 miles a year, with per-mile penalties above that. If your driving regularly exceeds those limits, whether for client visits, property showings, or house calls, buying typically avoids costly overage charges and gives you more flexibility.
